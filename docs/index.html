1 Introduction

Resource managers aim to maintain a delicate balance between resource extraction and ecological resilience. In fisheries, common management tools include spatial protection, gear limitations, size or slot limits, and seasonal closures. Spatial protection or marine protected areas (MPAs) involves closing specific geographic areas to some or all fishing activities. MPAs that protect critical habitats like coral reefs or spawning grounds help to ensure fisheries sustainability and can improve their productivity. In turn, healthy fish populations help to bolster ecosystem resilience that supports the local culture and economy.

In the U.S. Virgin Islands (USVI), there are multiple MPAs managed by either the USVI Department of Planning and Natural Resources (DPNR; territory) or the National Parks Service (NPS; federal) which encompass shallow water coral reefs. The no-take MPAs that prohibit extractive fishing activities include the St. Thomas East End Reserve (STEER) and Virgin Islands Coral Reef National Monument (VICR) in the northern USVI, as well as the St. Croix East End Marine Park (STXEEMP), Buck Island Reef National Monument (BUCK), and Salt River Bay National Historic Park and Ecological Preserve (SARI) on St. Croix.

To evaluate the effectiveness of MPAs for reef fishes targeted by the fishery, monitoring data are needed from both within the no-take MPAs and from the surrounding reef tract open to extractive fishing activities. The National Coral Reef Monitoring Program (NCRMP) conducts standardized reef fish surveys across the USVI reef tract, providing a solid framework for comparing and contextualizing the status of fishery-targeted reef fishes and the broader reef fish community (Feeley et al., 2025).

This report shows results from inside and outside the no-take MPAs including population-level estimates of relative density, occurrence, and length frequency distributions for economically valuable fish species of interest to marine spatial planners and resource managers.

2 Methods

In 2025, reef fish surveys at no-take MPAs and at sites throughout the USVI both used NCRMP’s sampling design and method. Non-extractive reef fish surveys occurred on hard-bottom reef habitats using a stratified-random, one-stage sampling design within 50 m × 50 m grid cells to ensure representative sampling across depth and rugosity strata (i.e., different habitats) (Ault et al., 2021; NOAA CRCP, 2025). Surveys used a stationary-point-count method modified from Bohnsack and Bannerot (1986) where a two-diver team surveyed all fish within adjacent 15-m diameter cylinders centered on each diver. Within each cylinder, fishes were identified to the species level, tallied, and fork length was estimated to the nearest centimeter (NOAA CRCP, 2025).

2.1 Data Analyses

Data collected by the diver pair were averaged at the site level. Standard fish metrics, including relative density, occurrence, and length composition, were evaluated for a suite of selected individual fish species (table 3). Computational formulas of standard metrics for a single-stage stratified random sampling design are modified from (Smith et al., 2011) and provided in detail in (Grove et al., 2021) and (Bryan et al., 2016). Fish analysis scripts are open source and available through the NCRMP Fish R package (Ganz & Blondeau, 2015).

2.1.1 St. Thomas and St. John

In St. Thomas and St. John, we evaluated reef fishes from three distinct areas: (1) St. Thomas East End Reserves (STEER), a no-take MPA managed by DPNR; (2) Virgin Islands Coral Reef National Monument (VICR), a no-take MPA managed by NPS; and (3) Open, areas of the reef tract where extractive fishing is permitted. Data were broken down by habit type and depth. Shallow strata are sites less than 12m in depth, and deep 12-99m in depth. To allow for direct comparisons, deep and shallow strata were combined within the habitat types bedrock, scattered coral and rock, and pavement to ensure an adequate sample size for analyses.

2.1.2 St. Croix

In St. Croix, we evaluated reef fishes from three distinct areas: (1) St. Croix East End Marine Park (STXEEMP), a no-take MPA managed by DPNR; (2) Buck Island Reef National Monument (BUCK) and Salt River Bay National Historic Park and Ecological Preserve (SARI), two no-take MPAs managed by NPS; and (3) Open, areas of the reef tract where extractive fishing is permitted. To allow for direct comparisons, the NCRMP and BUCK strata were restricted to the shallow strata found within STXEEMP and SARI (i.e., shallow aggregate, shallow patch, and shallow pavement). Strata with limited surveys were combined to achieve an adequate sample size for analyses (i.e., shallow scattered coral and rock + shallow pavement, shallow bedrock + shallow aggregate reef).

2.2 Species

Economically valuable species targeted by USVI fisheries were selected for analyses. Difficult to distiguish species, porgies, were combined.

3 Results

3.1 Density

Relative density is an index or relative measure of population density that is commonly used in fisheries surveys. Relative density is a comparative measure of density across locations or years, rather than an absolute count that is impractical due to the scale and complexity of the marine environment. Densities shown here represent NCRMP’s fish survey area (177m2) ± standard error.

NCRMP surveys capture a snapshot of coral reef fish populations within no-take MPAs and the surrounding reef tract. Since it is common for fish populations to fluctuate annually, whenever possible, resource managers should evaluate multiple years of fish density data to inform and assess management efforts. Consistently higher or increasing densities over time within no-take MPAs suggests these areas are effectively protecting critical habitat for fishery-targeted species.

3.2 Occurrence

Monitoring species occurrence, or how often a species is detected in surveys, offers valuable insight into its distribution. This metric provides presence data regardless of abundance, which helps to identify whether a species is widespread or rare. For resource managers, species occurrence can be used as a bioindicator of overall ecosystem health and to evaluate the success of no-take marine protected areas. A consistently higher occurrence of a fish species, especially of a fishery-target species, within a no-take MPA can serve as an indicator of management success. Error bars represent ± standard error.

3.3 Length Frequency

Length compositions provide a detailed description of a fish’s population structure. These highly informative figures can show the length at which a species recruits to the coral reef from their nursery habitat, the length classes that are selected by local recreational and commercial fisheries, and the effectiveness of fisheries management regulations. Successful marine spatial protection can yield greater densities and larger sizes of fishery-target species.

## Density
Relative density is an index or relative measure of population density that is commonly used in fisheries surveys. Relative density is a comparative measure of density across locations or years, rather than an absolute count that is impractical due to the scale and complexity of the marine environment. Densities shown here represent NCRMP’s fish survey area (177m2) ± standard error.

NCRMP surveys capture a snapshot of coral reef fish populations within no-take MPAs and the surrounding reef tract. Since it is common for fish populations to fluctuate annually, whenever possible, resource managers should evaluate multiple years of fish density data to inform and assess management efforts. Consistently higher or increasing densities over time within no-take MPAs suggests these areas are effectively protecting critical habitat for fishery-targeted species.

## Occurrence

Monitoring species occurrence, or how often a species is detected in surveys, offers valuable insight into its distribution. This metric provides presence data regardless of abundance, which helps to identify whether a species is widespread or rare. For resource managers, species occurrence can be used as a bioindicator of overall ecosystem health and to evaluate the success of no-take marine protected areas. A consistently higher occurrence of a fish species, especially of a fishery-target species, within a no-take MPA can serve as an indicator of management success. Error bars represent ± standard error.

## Length Frequency

Length compositions provide a detailed description of a fish’s population structure. These highly informative figures can show the length at which a species recruits to the coral reef from their nursery habitat, the length classes that are selected by local recreational and commercial fisheries, and the effectiveness of fisheries management regulations. Successful marine spatial protection can yield greater densities and larger sizes of fishery-target species.

Relative length frequency by fork length (cm) bin is shown for each species, sampling domain (St. Croix and St. Thomas/St. John), and no-take MPA (i.e., territory and federal).
